What gets left behind.

Rodents bring more than noise. Over weeks they leave droppings, urine stains, torn nest material and trampled insulation that can lose its insulating value. A long-running infestation can add up to a lot of waste in a small space.

Cleanup does not make sense until the animals are out and every entry is closed. Otherwise the work is wasted. That is why this job sits after trapping and sealing in the usual order.

The safe way to handle waste.

According to the CDC, dry droppings are not to be swept or vacuumed, because that lifts dust that may carry germs. The method it gives: ventilate, glove up, soak the waste with disinfectant, wait several minutes, wipe up and bag it all.

  • Open the space and let it air before anyone goes in.
  • Wear gloves and protective gear suited to the space.
  • Wet waste thoroughly instead of brushing it.
  • Close debris inside bags and place it with the regular garbage.
  • Wash hands and tools afterward.

The technician follows the same method and adds equipment suited to tight spaces, such as filtered vacuums for use after wetting. Where waste is heavy, the area is cleaned in sections.

Attics and crawlspaces differ.

Attics are dry, hot in summer and dusty. Waste sits in insulation, and removal often means bagging batts or vacuuming loose-fill material carefully. Crawlspaces are damp and low, and soil, vapor barriers and ducts all add steps.

Wet Pacific Northwest weather matters in both. Damp crawlspaces hold odors and speed mold, so ventilation and moisture are checked while cleaning. If the wetness is a bigger problem than the animals, we say so and suggest the right trade.

Planning the visit.

Tell us how to get in, how much headroom there is and whether the space has lighting. Hatches, access doors and ladders should be clear. If the attic holds stored items, moving them first speeds the job and keeps your belongings clean.

If the house has a garage attic or a crawl vent near an exterior wall, show us those spots and we will check them with the rest. Odors that persist after cleaning may point to a dead animal that still needs locating.

Tell us when you call.

  • How much headroom the space offers.
  • Whether insulation looks flattened, stained or torn up.
  • Whether anyone in the home has trouble breathing.

Before we arrive: cleaning rodent waste safely

Tidying before the appointment? Go in this order and stop whenever a step seems unclear.

  • Put away the compressed air, the broom and the dry rag whenever you find pellets or stained nesting.
  • Fresh air comes first for any shut space, and half an hour is the usual figure.
  • Either disposable or reusable rubber gloves will do, as long as you wash both the gloves and your hands.
  • Sequence counts: wet it, rest it for five minutes or so, then wipe, which keeps dust down while the product acts.
  • Seal the bag, and keep skin away from the animal.
  • We say nothing about anyone's health. If fever, aches or labored breathing come after contact with waste, see a physician.

Cleanup questions, answered.

Why can't I just vacuum it myself?

Dry droppings crumble into fine dust, and ordinary vacuums can blow it back into the air. Wet cleanup, gloves and sealed bags are the safer route.

Does all the insulation have to go?

Not always. Lightly soiled areas may be treated, while heavily fouled or flattened sections are usually removed and replaced.

When should cleanup be done?

After trapping and sealing, so new animals cannot undo the work. Doing it first only invites a fresh mess.

Does cleanup remove smells?

It removes the source of most odors. A dead animal, if there is one, is dealt with on the odor page, and lingering smell fades as the space dries and airs.

Is a crawlspace cleanup different from an attic one?

Yes. Moisture, low headroom and soil change the tools and the order of work, so the visit is planned around where the sign is.
