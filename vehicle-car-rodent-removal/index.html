What draws rodents to vehicles.

An engine stays warm for hours after it is shut off, and the space around it is dark, dry and sheltered. Insulation on wiring, foam in the air filter housing and cabin filters offer nest material. Add a gap through the firewall or a vent, and the vehicle becomes a den.

The risk is highest when a vehicle sits. A truck parked for a week, a camper stored for a season or a boat trailer left by the road can all attract animals, and in a wet climate, dry cover under a hood is hard to resist.

Where to look.

  • Under the hood, around the air filter housing and on top of the engine cover.
  • In the cabin air filter compartment behind the glove box.
  • Along the firewall and in the wheel wells.
  • Inside the trunk or truck bed, especially under liners.
  • In a camper's cabinets, wiring chase and propane compartment.

Gloves and a flashlight are enough for a first look. Do not blow out debris or hose out the engine bay, because dust and droppings can become airborne. The CDC advises damp cleanup for droppings and nests.

What damage looks like.

Chewed insulation on wires, loose connectors, blown fuses and warning lights are the usual signs. A burning smell or a car that will not start after sitting can point to a problem. Do not drive a vehicle you suspect is damaged.

We do not repair vehicles. After removal, a mechanic or auto electrician should inspect the wiring and replace damaged parts. We can describe what we found to help them.

Where the vehicle is kept.

A vehicle parked beside a woodpile, a brush patch or an unsealed garage is at risk. In Silverdale, a driveway or storage lot near trees or green ground can be as risky as a garage. If you keep a car, truck or boat in a storage area, tell us what it is near.

Moving the vehicle for a few days can help break the pattern. Running the engine often, parking in an open area and keeping the hood latched are simple steps. See garage and basement control for the indoor side.

The visit and what follows.

The technician inspects the vehicle, removes animals and nests, and notes damage. Traps may be set near the parking spot if animals keep returning. Trapping and removal explains how devices are placed and checked.

If a vehicle has a strong odor after removal, odor control can help find the source. Prevention is the last step: keep food out, store the vehicle off wet ground where possible, and check the engine bay often.

Mention these first.

  • How long the vehicle was parked and where.
  • Any warning lights, strange smells or fuses that blow.
  • Whether it is a car, truck, camper, boat or trailer.
  • Where the vehicle is kept and what is nearby.

Prepare for the visit.

  • Stay out of attic and crawlspace until we arrive, because moving insulation puts dust in the air.
  • Leave nests and burrows unpoked, because the residents may only move deeper.
  • Find the attic hatch and the crawlspace door now so nobody searches later.
  • A vehicle with signs of nesting can be rolled outside, if that is easy.

Before you phone: dealing with droppings

This list repeats federal advice in everyday words, and it holds for any household whether or not it ever hires us.

  • The first rule is simple: dry waste is never brushed or vacuumed.
  • Fresh air comes first for any shut space, and half an hour is the usual figure.
  • Gloves go on at the start, and handwashing closes the job.
  • Dampen each pellet and stain with disinfectant and let it stand about five minutes before picking anything up.
  • Use a bag that seals rather than an open bin, and put gloves or a tool between you and any carcass.
  • Questions about your health belong with medical professionals, and symptoms are a reason to tell them what happened.

Vehicle questions, answered.

Why do rodents nest in cars?

Engine bays are warm, dark and sheltered, and wiring insulation, filters and cabin vents supply both material and ways in. A vehicle parked for days or weeks is an especially good target.

How do I know if rodents have been in my car?

Look for nest material under the hood or in the air box, droppings on the engine cover, chewed wires, a musty smell from the vents or a warning light that appeared without explanation.

Should I start the engine?

Not if you find nest material. Starting an engine with debris in the air intake or around belts can cause damage or a fire.

Who fixes chewed wiring?

A mechanic or auto electrician. We take out the animals and nests, record what we see and suggest how to prevent a repeat.

Do you handle boats, campers and trailers?

Yes. Boats, campers, trailers and equipment kept outside or in storage can host rodents too, and the same inspection and removal steps apply.
