Why the garage is a first stop.

A garage offers what rodents want: shelter, quiet and material for nests. Cardboard, insulation, fabric and paper are all within reach, and a gap beneath the door gives easy access. By the time anyone notices, a nest may already be in place.

In a rainy climate the draw is stronger. Animals look for dry cover, and a closed garage is often the driest spot on a property. Mice and rats can move in from woods or brush at the lot's edge, and a stack of totes makes a ready-made shelter.

Where the technician looks.

  • The overhead door bottom seal and the side tracks.
  • The seam between wall base and slab.
  • Utility penetrations, such as gas, water and electrical lines.
  • Spaces behind shelving and workbenches.
  • Gaps near the attic hatch or the ceiling above.
  • Basement window wells, vents and sump areas.

The inspection takes as long as it takes. Animals leave tidy trails, and following them reveals the real entry. A single gap at a door corner often explains a whole problem.

Wiring, freezers and heat sources.

Warm things attract animals, and so do the cords around them. A spare freezer, a water heater closet or a workbench with power tools can attract a nest, and chewed insulation on a cord is a fire hazard. Report any burning smell or tripped breaker right away.

If you find damaged wiring, do not touch it. Call, and mention it clearly so the visit can include a note for an electrician where needed. The repair page explains how damage gets scoped.

Wet-season storage habits.

Silverdale sits near Dyes Inlet in a part of the world where long wet spells are normal, and garages often become storage for outdoor gear. Wet gear in cardboard is a magnet, so switching to sealed plastic totes on shelves helps. Keep bags of seed and pet food off the floor entirely.

If you keep a boat or camper in the garage, check the cabin and engine area after any long gap. The vehicle page explains what to look for.

How the job finishes.

Animals are trapped out first. Then exclusion work closes the openings, and any soiled areas are cleaned. A return visit checks the traps, the seals and your shelves. If the basement or garage has a persistent smell, odor control looks for the source.

Have these ready.

  • How the garage door seals and whether it is attached to the house.
  • Stored items stacked against walls.
  • Any spare freezer, workbench or hobby gear.
  • Whether the lower level ever feels damp.

Ahead of the visit.

  • Open a lane to the garage wall, the sink cabinet and the attic hatch.
  • Gnawed cartons stay closed, because shaking one out lofts the contents.
  • Snap traps belong well away from toddlers and dogs.
  • Name the room that smells strongest and describe the smell.

A safety checklist drawn from CDC advice

Walk through this routine each time pellets, stains or shredded nest scraps appear indoors.

  • A shop vacuum feels efficient but can blow tiny particles right back into the room.
  • Budget half an hour of open doors before the cleanup even starts.
  • Germs are too small to see, so gloves go on before you start and soap and water finish the job.
  • Sequence counts: wet it, rest it for five minutes or so, then wipe, which keeps dust down while the product acts.
  • A knotted bag goes in the household trash, and a dead rodent should never be picked up bare handed.
  • We say nothing about anyone's health. If fever, aches or labored breathing come after contact with waste, see a physician.

Questions about garages and basements.

Why do rodents like garages?

Garages are dry, rarely disturbed and full of nesting material. A gap at the door or along the wall base gives easy access, and totes against a wall hide them.

Is my attached garage a route into the house?

It can be. Pipes, wires and wall gaps often connect the garage to living space, and the technician checks the shared wall carefully.

What about a damp lower level?

Moisture invites animals and speeds mold. We note damp spots and suggest the right trade where wetness is the larger problem.

Should I move stored items?

If you can do it safely, pull items away from walls and group them on shelves. Wear gloves and avoid shaking out boxes that show droppings.

Can you protect stored boats, kayaks or gear?

We check for entries and nests around them. Storing gear in sealed totes and off the floor reduces the risk, and the technician can suggest placement.
