What we can offer is practical. A home on a hillside, or on any slope, shares certain risks regardless of its exact location. Tell us your address on the call, and the technician plans the visit around what is in front of the house, not around a label.

Reading a sloped lot.

Water follows gravity, and so do rodents that want a dry den. If your lot slopes toward the house, rain and runoff can collect near the foundation, soften soil and hide burrows. Mice and rats may then reach vents, sleeves and sill plates with little effort.

A quick check after a heavy rain shows where water pools. Look for gaps at the base of siding, wet patches near crawlspace vents and soil that has washed away from a footing. Report what you see, because those spots are the first places a technician examines.

  • Downspouts that dump water against the foundation.
  • Soil washed away from a vent or sill.
  • Retaining walls with gaps or weep holes at the base.
  • Stairs and decks with open space beneath them.

Crawlspaces and daylight basements.

Hillside houses often include a crawlspace or a lower level cut into the slope, though we cannot say how any one house is built. These spaces are damp, dim and rarely visited. A torn vent screen or a gap around a pipe can let animals in and leave them undisturbed for months.

If your house has one, open the access door and look in with a flashlight before the visit, but do not crawl in while droppings are visible. Note any torn insulation, flattened ducts or a musty smell. The cleanup page explains what happens after the animals are removed.

Trees, roofs and soffits.

Tall trees bring shade, needles and cover. They also drop limbs onto roofs and stretch branches toward the eaves. A rat can walk a limb to a soffit and slip through a gap that looks too small to matter.

Limbs touching the roof are worth trimming or reporting. Where the work is large, we suggest an arborist and do not attempt it ourselves. Exclusion work then closes the openings that remain.

Garages, outbuildings and storage.

Slopes often push storage toward the garage or a shed. Cardboard, firewood and seasonal gear stacked against a wall add nesting material and shelter. In wet months, moving them onto shelves and into sealed totes helps more than most people expect.

If a shed sits at the edge of a wooded area, check it first. Animals often use sheds as a base before reaching the house, and the yard page describes what to look for.

What to tell the dispatcher.

Tell us the street or the nearest cross street, whether the house has a crawlspace, basement or slab, and any retaining walls, decks or limbs near the roof. Say too whether the lot slopes toward or away from the house. With that, the first visit can begin in the right place.

First small steps.

  • A car with nesting material under the hood stays parked until it has been checked.
  • Carry lawn seed, pet kibble and birdseed into the house for now.
  • Keep any chewed object in a bag for the technician.
  • Know the location of the main electrical panel, since a wire may have been gnawed.

Before you sweep, spray or bag: safety steps

Walk through this routine each time pellets, stains or shredded nest scraps appear indoors.

  • Put away the compressed air, the broom and the dry rag whenever you find pellets or stained nesting.
  • Prop open windows and doors, step outside for around thirty minutes, and let the air exchange.
  • Germs are too small to see, so gloves go on before you start and soap and water finish the job.
  • Saturate the waste, leave it for five minutes, then collect it on towels.
  • Wipes and debris share one closed bag that joins the ordinary garbage, and hands stay covered around any body.
  • For the next few weeks, notice how you feel, and if fever or breathlessness shows up, tell a doctor exactly what you handled.

Are hillside homes more prone to rodents?

Not by themselves. Slopes can add drainage problems, and wooded edges add cover, though a flat lot may have gaps too. The inspection settles it.
