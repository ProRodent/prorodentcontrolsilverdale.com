Noises, odors and mess.

People often wait because a clue seems minor. Treat any of these as a reason to ask and not as a reason to wait.

  • Sounds that travel along the inside of a wall.
  • Droppings lined up behind baseboards or scattered through attic insulation.
  • Kibble or birdseed bags with chewed corners.
  • Holes dug next to a foundation, shed or deck.
  • A stench that builds when the day gets warm.

Why the calendar matters.

Long rainy spells push animals toward dry cover. A calm summer can turn noisy by November, and a slow walk around the outside before the wet season sets in costs very little.

Outbuildings and long-parked vehicles.

A shed, a workshop or a detached garage can serve as a base camp. Animals settle there first, then work toward the house. If you own one, tell us whether it has a slab or a dirt floor, and whether it holds feed, seed or firewood.

A truck or trailer that sits unused for weeks is another favorite. Lift the hood before starting it, and read the vehicle page for signs of a nest. If you find one, wait for guidance before turning the key.

Before you phone: dealing with droppings

General information only, taken from federal public health pages, and your own doctor outranks it on health questions.

  • A vacuum or leaf blower kicks up the exact material you are trying to avoid, so keep both away from dry waste.
  • Air the space first. Open whatever opens, then wait about thirty minutes.
  • Gloves go on at the start, and handwashing closes the job.
  • Think of three beats, soak then pause then wipe, where the pause lasts around five minutes.
  • All that you wiped up gets sealed in a bag before it reaches a bin, and a dead animal calls for gloves.
  • Fever, aches or breathing trouble in the following weeks call for a doctor, who should hear that rodent waste was close by. Our trade is pests, not medicine.
