What rodents can damage.

Chewing keeps a rodent's teeth in check, so they gnaw on anything within reach. In houses that means insulation, duct wrap, vent screens, wire coverings, soft wood, drywall corners and stored fabric. Over weeks the damage adds up.

Wet climates make some damage worse. Soaked insulation loses value, rotted wood is easier to chew, and a chewed vent screen lets in rain as well as animals. Fixing the cause comes first, then the damage.

Scoping damage honestly.

The technician walks the property and records what is damaged, with photographs. Each item is described in plain words: what happened, whether it needs replacing, and what the effect is if left alone.

  • Insulation: flattened, stained or tunneled.
  • Ducts: torn foil wrap or holes.
  • Vents and screens: gnawed edges or missing mesh.
  • Wires: chewed insulation, a job for an electrician.
  • Wood and drywall: gnawed corners, entry holes.

Where damage may be hazardous, such as exposed wiring, we say so and recommend a licensed electrician. We do not handle wiring ourselves.

Why order matters.

Repair follows trapping, sealing and, where needed, cleanup. Doing it earlier risks repeating the work. A sealed, clean space gets repairs that last.

If you need to act quickly, such as covering a hole in a vent or protecting exposed wires, we explain temporary steps that do not trap animals inside.

Local conditions.

A damp, wooded setting near the water can make wood and insulation more vulnerable, so we check for moisture while we scope repairs. If a leak or standing water is the bigger problem, we recommend fixing it first. The technician stays with what we can honestly do.

Silverdale homes differ in age, and we never assume the condition of a building before we look. A new house can have gnawed vents, and an older one may be sound. The inspection decides.

Points to raise.

  • What looks chewed, stained or torn.
  • Photos of damage taken from a safe distance.
  • Whether wiring or ducts appear affected.
  • Whether a recent leak or storm made things worse.

Before anyone arrives.

  • Make sure the side gate opens, and say whether a dog runs loose in the yard.
  • Ivy and shrubs touching the house can stay as they are until the visit.
  • Put a flashlight where you can grab it for dark corners.
  • Take pictures of burrows, pellets and nests before any are moved or covered over.

A safety checklist for droppings

This list repeats federal advice in everyday words, and it holds for any household whether or not it ever hires us.

  • Whatever the mess looks like, resist dry methods and start with water or disinfectant.
  • Closed-up rooms hold stale air, so open the doors and windows and stay out for about half an hour.
  • As a minimum, work in rubber gloves and wash well when you finish.
  • Disinfect before collecting. The wet waste should sit around five minutes first.
  • Wipes and debris share one closed bag that joins the ordinary garbage, and hands stay covered around any body.
  • We say nothing about anyone's health. If fever, aches or labored breathing come after contact with waste, see a physician.

Repair questions, answered.

What do rodents damage most often?

Insulation, duct wrap, vent covers, wire insulation, trim and stored items. Damage to wiring and ducts matters most because of fire risk and lost efficiency.

Can I fix it myself?

Small cosmetic items, perhaps. Anything touching wiring, gas or structure should go to a qualified tradesperson.

Why does repair come after sealing?

Rodents return to an unsealed house. Fixing damage first would just give them something new to chew.

Will insurance cover it?

That depends on your policy. Ask your insurer, and we can provide photos and a list of what we found.

How do you price repairs?

Each repair is scoped and priced as its own item after the walk-through, so you see what you are paying for.
