What a rat problem tends to look like.

Rats are larger and braver than mice. Their droppings are about the size of a big grain of rice or larger, tooth marks on wood and plastic are broad, and runways appear as greasy lines along a wall or fence. Noise tends to be heavy steps and dragging overhead after dark.

Two species dominate across the Pacific Northwest. Norway rats dig and stay low, near foundations and beneath decks, while roof rats climb and favor the upper parts of a building. The species chosen decides where traps go and which openings get sealed first.

The yard is where the trail begins.

A rat rarely appears inside without a route from outdoors. Limbs touching the roof, cables, fences and dense vegetation along the foundation all work as bridges. A lot with tall trees or thick brush can offer many of these at once.

If your property backs onto green ground such as the Clear Creek Trail corridor or the woods beside Silverdale Waterfront Park, tell us which side of the lot faces it. That side is walked first, and it often shows a path worn into the ground.

  • Piles of firewood, lumber or pallets resting against walls.
  • Compost bins without a base, and fruit left on the ground.
  • Bird feed spilled beneath a feeder.
  • Chicken or pet food stored in open bags.

Trapping that matches the animal.

Rats are wary, so traps are set where they already travel and are often left unbaited for a day so the animals grow used to them. Placement along a wall or fence beats placement in the open, and a station near a burrow does the most work.

Everything is logged by location so that no trap is forgotten when the job ends. Details of the trapping process are on the trapping and removal page.

Closing what a rat can use.

Rats widen a gap by gnawing it, so foam and wood are poor choices. Our technician uses flashing, hardware cloth and sealant around vents, soffit edges, pipe sleeves and the base of garage doors. Bigger openings may take a second visit, and the exclusion page covers the materials.

Timing and follow-up.

Rat work usually takes more than one visit. After the first round of trapping and sealing, we come back to check catches, test seals and decide what cleanup is needed. If insulation or ducting is soiled, cleanup for attics and crawlspaces comes after the entries are shut.

Points to raise.

  • When and where the rat was seen or heard.
  • Tall trees, fences, decks or a shed at the back.
  • The droppings you found, and about how big.
  • A compost pile, chicken feed or a bird feeder nearby.

While you wait for us.

  • A car with nesting material under the hood stays parked until it has been checked.
  • Carry lawn seed, pet kibble and birdseed into the house for now.
  • Keep any chewed object in a bag for the technician.
  • Know the location of the main electrical panel, since a wire may have been gnawed.

Safety steps for waste and nests

General information only, taken from federal public health pages, and your own doctor outranks it on health questions.

  • Put away the compressed air, the broom and the dry rag whenever you find pellets or stained nesting.
  • Closed-up rooms hold stale air, so open the doors and windows and stay out for about half an hour.
  • Keep your hands covered for the whole task, then scrub.
  • Dampen each pellet and stain with disinfectant and let it stand about five minutes before picking anything up.
  • Droppings and wipes go in a bag that closes tight, and skin never meets a dead animal.
  • We say nothing about anyone's health. If fever, aches or labored breathing come after contact with waste, see a physician.

The reason rat work begins outdoors.

  • The route runs outside.

    Rats generally reach a house along a fence, cable, limb or burrow, so the inspection starts in the yard and moves to the attic after that.

  • Wider gaps, stronger teeth.

    Half an inch is enough for a rat, and it can chew through soft materials, so we seal with metal and wire mesh.

  • Food sources identified.

    Feeders, compost, pet bowls and windfall fruit each get a plain suggestion in a short list, so nothing stays a mystery.

Rat questions from Silverdale callers.

Rat or mouse: how can I tell?

Size is the best clue. Rat droppings are as big as a large rice grain or bigger, and mouse droppings are small with pointed ends. Burrows along foundations and fruit with deep gnaw marks also suggest rats.

Do rats live in attics around here?

They can. Roof rats climb with ease and may use limbs or wires to reach the roofline, and thumping overhead at dusk is a common report.

Should I plug a hole I found?

Hold off until the technician comes. Closing a hole with an animal still inside can drive it deeper into the house, and a quick plug is often gnawed right out again.

Do rats come with blackberry thickets and brush?

Dense brush gives rats cover close to a house, so it is checked along with the foundation. Cutting it back helps, and we say which patches matter most.

Will a rat visit take longer than a mouse visit?

Often. The yard, the roofline and the attic all get examined, so plan for a longer walk-through and possibly a return trip to close the larger openings.
