Why animals die inside walls.

An animal that eats poison tends to retreat somewhere quiet before it dies, for example a wall void, an attic corner or the crawlspace. Others become trapped in a space they cannot leave. The odor starts within days, and in a closed house it can travel.

The odor is distinctive. It is usually described as sharp, sweet or heavy, and it grows stronger on warm, humid days. Flies are a common clue, because they find a carcass quickly.

Finding the source.

The technician begins by plotting where the odor is strongest and where flies congregate. Temperature changes, ceiling stains and sounds in ducts add clues, and a small inspection camera can see into voids without cutting large openings.

  • Walk each room and compare smell strength.
  • Check ceilings for stains or dark spots.
  • Inspect the attic, crawlspace and under-floor ducts.
  • Look near pipes, vents and heat sources.
  • Check behind appliances and under built-in cabinets.

Sometimes the source is hard to reach. In that case, the technician explains options, such as a small access hole, and the likely cost before cutting anything.

Handling the animal safely.

A carcass can carry germs, so the CDC recommends gloves, a soaking of the area with disinfectant and everything sealed in bags. Keep kids and pets away. If something must be moved, use gloves and a tool, and wash your hands afterward.

In a wet climate, damp insulation holds odor. If material beneath or around the animal is soaked, it may need to be removed. That is scoped separately, and cleanup explains how.

Treating the area.

After removal, the space is cleaned and treated so that bacteria and odor do not remain. Ventilation helps, and a damp space may need a fan or dehumidifier. Odor can fade over days or weeks, and the technician says what to expect.

Prevention for the next wet season.

Once the animal is gone, the way in has to close or the cycle repeats. Exclusion work finds and seals entries, and trapping removes any survivors. Skip poison, since it is the most common reason an animal dies where nobody can reach it.

Good to have in hand.

  • The room where the smell is worst.
  • Whether flies are showing up indoors.
  • When the smell started and whether it is getting stronger.
  • Any recent trapping or poison use in the house.

Ahead of our arrival.

  • A car with nesting material under the hood stays parked until it has been checked.
  • Carry lawn seed, pet kibble and birdseed into the house for now.
  • Keep any chewed object in a bag for the technician.
  • Know the location of the main electrical panel, since a wire may have been gnawed.

Safety guidance from the CDC cleanup page

Tidying before the appointment? Go in this order and stop whenever a step seems unclear.

  • Put away the compressed air, the broom and the dry rag whenever you find pellets or stained nesting.
  • After weeks of being sealed, a space deserves thirty minutes with every door wide.
  • Germs are too small to see, so gloves go on before you start and soap and water finish the job.
  • Hands stay off droppings until they are soaked and have sat for about five minutes.
  • Use a bag that seals rather than an open bin, and put gloves or a tool between you and any carcass.
  • Questions about your health belong with medical professionals, and symptoms are a reason to tell them what happened.

Odor questions, answered.

How long will the smell last?

It varies with the animal's size, the temperature and the humidity of the space. The odor may fade within a couple of weeks once the source is removed, though in damp places it can hang on.

Can I find it myself?

Sometimes. Follow the smell, look for flies and check likely spots. Do not cut walls open blindly, because the smell may travel along ducts and joists.

Will air fresheners help?

They cover the smell briefly and do not remove the cause. The source has to come out first.

Is it dangerous?

A decomposing animal can carry germs. Use gloves, air the space and avoid disturbing it. The CDC page linked below covers safe cleanup.

Does poison cause this?

It can. An animal that eats bait may die in a wall, which is one reason we prefer trapping.
